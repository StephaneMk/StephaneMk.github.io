Where is competition lacking in public procurement?

My personal data project on French public procurement: a complete data pipeline, from the open data file to a dashboard refreshed every night.

By Moïse Stéphane KINYOK18 November 2026
  • #data
  • #sql
  • #power bi
  • #elasticsearch

Every day, the French State, local authorities and hospitals buy works, supplies and services with public money. The basic rule is competitive tendering: several companies submit a bid, and the buyer chooses the best one. When an open tender receives only one bid, the buyer has nothing to compare it with. The European Commission tracks this single-bid rate as a risk indicator.

Since 2018, French public buyers have published the essential data of their contracts, the DECP (données essentielles de la commande publique), as open data on data.gouv.fr. I wanted to use it to answer a simple question: where, with which buyers and in which sectors is competition lacking, and is the data reliable enough to say so?

The result is live: the project website (in French), recalculated every night, and the source code on GitLab.

A complete data pipeline

I did not want to stop at a notebook. The project follows the path data takes in a company, from the source to the indicator:

  1. Collection and quality: downloading the DECP since January 2024 and five cleaning rules in Python (pandas). Over 840,000 contracts are tracked today.
  2. Ingestion: a Logstash pipeline loads the contracts into a three-node Elasticsearch cluster, on a lab of six virtual machines built with Vagrant.
  3. SQL model: a PostgreSQL database with a star schema (one fact table, four dimensions), competition views and checks for six types of anomaly.
  4. Reporting: a two-page Power BI report, with twelve DAX measures that give exactly the same figures as the SQL.
  5. Anomaly detection: Elastic ML jobs that compare each buyer with the others, week after week.
  6. Automatic refresh: every night, the file is downloaded again, cleaned, recalculated and checked before being published on the site.

I ran the project in six Scrum sprints in Jira, with a README for each ticket and a data dictionary.

What the data shows

  • One in five contracts put out to tender receives only one bid, close to the European benchmark.
  • The lack of competition is above all an island and overseas issue: Corsica and Guadeloupe are above 30%.
  • A regional rate can come down to a single buyer. In Provence-Alpes-Côte d’Azur, the City of Marseille stood at 66.7% single bids when I analysed the data on 12 November 2026.
  • Services are more affected than works: public administration services, culture and leisure, transport and insurance are above 35%.

I keep one rule of caution throughout the project: a high rate or an anomaly is a signal to check, never proof of wrongdoing. Display thresholds (500 contracts for a region, 100 for a buyer) also avoid drawing conclusions from too little data.

Two complementary methods

I compared two ways of spotting risks. SQL rules are explainable and exact: they find a lack of competition and dependence on a single supplier, but only what you already know to look for. Elastic Machine Learning compares each buyer with the others: it flagged two care homes declaring 25 and 29 billion euros of contracts, data entry errors that my rules had missed.

The strongest signal is the one both methods share: public bodies in Marseille stand out in both the SQL and the ML results.

The limits, stated clearly

Public data has its flaws, and a good indicator shows them. About one in five contracts put out to tender does not report its number of bids and is therefore left out of the calculation. This reporting rate varies by region, and the site shows it next to the single-bid rate. An awarded amount is also a ceiling set in the contract, not actual spending.

What I take away

This project let me practise everything I enjoy about the Data / Business Analyst role: starting from a concrete question, making the data reliable before looking at it, building indicators that give the same result whatever the tool, and presenting them so that a non-specialist reader can understand them.
